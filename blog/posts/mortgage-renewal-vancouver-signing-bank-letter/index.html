What Happens When You Just Sign Your Bank's Mortgage Renewal Letter in Vancouver

September 14, 2026 · Mack Chaisson, Licensed Mortgage Broker Renewals

Every year, thousands of Metro Vancouver homeowners open an envelope or email from their bank, see a renewal offer for their mortgage, and sign it back within a few days. It feels simple, responsible even. But for most borrowers carrying a mortgage on a Burnaby condo, a Richmond townhouse, or a detached home in East Van, that signature is one of the most expensive passive decisions they will make. Here is what is actually happening when you do nothing at renewal time.

The Renewal Letter Is Not Their Best Offer

Your bank knows something useful about you at renewal time: you are busy, the process of switching feels complicated, and inertia is powerful. The rate printed on that renewal letter is almost always a posted rate, not the discounted rate they would offer a new customer walking in from the street, and certainly not what a broker can access across dozens of lenders.

Banks are regulated institutions with shareholders. Their renewal letters are designed to retain you at a margin that works for them. There is nothing sinister about it, but there is also nothing in that letter that rewards your loyalty as a long-standing borrower. The assumption is that you will sign.

In Metro Vancouver, where mortgage balances are often substantial relative to the rest of the country, even a small difference in rate compounds meaningfully over a five-year term. That gap between the letter rate and what is actually available in the market is worth understanding before you commit.

What "Doing Nothing" Actually Triggers

If you miss your renewal deadline entirely, most lenders in Canada will roll your mortgage into an open mortgage at a posted variable rate. Open mortgages allow you to leave anytime without penalty, which sounds flexible, but they typically carry a noticeably higher rate than a standard closed term. You end up paying for flexibility you probably did not want or need.

Even if you sign the renewal letter on time, you are agreeing to another full term, usually one to five years, at whatever rate and conditions were offered. That locks in an outcome you never compared against anything else. In a market like Metro Vancouver where carrying costs are already significant, that is a meaningful decision made by default rather than by choice.

What you give up when you sign without shopping

  • The ability to switch lenders at no penalty. Mortgage renewal is one of the only moments in your term when you can move to a different lender, often with no penalty at all, because your existing term has ended.
  • Access to broker-channel lenders. Many competitive lenders do not have retail branches in Vancouver. They exist only through the broker channel, which means your bank will never mention them.
  • A chance to restructure. Renewal is a natural point to revisit your amortization, payment schedule, or prepayment privileges based on how your life has changed since you first bought.
  • Negotiating leverage. Once you have signed, it is gone. Before you sign, especially with a competing offer in hand, lenders will often move.

When to Start, and Why Earlier Than You Think

Most lenders will let you lock in a renewal or a rate hold well before your maturity date, sometimes several months out. Starting the conversation early means you are not making a rushed decision, and if rates shift in your favour before your term ends, there may be options to capture that. If rates move the other way, a rate hold can protect you.

For homeowners in areas like South Surrey, Coquitlam, or anywhere else in Metro Vancouver where property values have shifted substantially over the past several years, renewal is also a good moment to revisit what your equity position looks like and whether that opens up different product options.

The short version: the renewal letter your bank sends is a starting point, not a final answer. Understanding what is actually available before you sign is the kind of review that takes one conversation and can shape the next several years of your mortgage costs.
